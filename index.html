    Two numbers, one report

    They measure different things. Mixing them up is how good managers lose a period arguing.

    Theoretical food cost

    What food should have cost, based on what we sold and what each recipe calls for. It assumes perfect portions, zero waste, accurate recipe costs, and every comp rung in.

    Recipe cost × items sold, divided by food sales

    Actual food cost

    What food did cost. It comes from invoices and inventory counts, not from recipe cards. It is the number that matches the money that left our account.

    Starting inventory plus purchases minus ending inventory, divided by food sales

    Variance

    Actual minus theoretical. Every point of variance is product we paid for that never became a sale. It is the size of the opportunity, not a grade on the software.

    Read the gap, not the software

    Theoretical is the floor we could hit if the kitchen ran perfectly. Actual is where we landed. The space between them is money we can still win back.

    Why theoretical still matters

    Theoretical is a diagnostic, not a destination. When the gap opens, it points at one of these.

    Portioning

    Heavy hands on a high volume item move the number fastest.

    Waste

    Overprep, dropped plates, expired product, remakes.

    Receiving

    Short cases, wrong weights, price increases nobody caught.

    Comps and voids

    Food that left the kitchen without a ticket behind it.

    Counting

    Different units, different shelves, different day each count.

    The system

    An outdated recipe or price. Real, fixable, and only one item on this list.

    The “system is wrong” trap

    Sometimes the system is wrong. That is worth fixing. It is not worth stopping for, because fixing the report never moved a dollar into the bank.

    Argue the number

    1. Theoretical looks off
    2. Meeting about recipe cards and software
    3. Nobody changes a portion or a par
    4. Same gap next period
    5. $0 back

    Work the number

    1. Theoretical looks off
    2. Flag the suspect item for a recipe check, then move on
    3. Walk the line, check the dock, audit the waste log
    4. Gap shrinks
    5. Money in the bank

    Fix the system

    Weekly task

    Flag the item, verify the recipe and current price, update it. This makes the report more accurate. It does not change what we paid.

    Manage actual

    Every shift

    Receiving, prep, portions, waste, comps, counts. This is the only work that changes the number on the deposit.

    Even when theoretical is wrong, actual is right about one thing: what we paid.

    Six levers, dock to plate

    Follow the food through the building. Every lever is something a manager controls on a shift.

    1. Receive it right

      Check weights, counts, and prices against the invoice before the driver leaves. A credit you never ask for is product you paid for and never got.

    2. Prep to par

      Prep what the day needs, not what feels safe. Overprep on Friday is waste on Sunday.

    3. Portion every plate

      Scoops, scales, and spec cards on the line. Small overportions on a top seller add up faster than anything else.

    4. Log every waste

      If it hits the trash, it hits the log. You cannot fix what you cannot see.

    5. Ring every comp

      Food that leaves without a ticket still shows up on the invoice.

    6. Count it the same way

      Same day, same order, same units, every count. A bad count makes both numbers lie.
